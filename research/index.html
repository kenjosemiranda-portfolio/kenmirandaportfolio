December 2025 · Conference paper

From Hook to Play: Mapping the Elements of “Vibe” that Drive Generation Alpha’s Short-Form Video Consumption

10th International Conference on Globalisation, Entrepreneurship and Emerging Economies (ICGEEE 2025), hosted by the Centre for Business & Economic Research (CBER)

University of London, United Kingdom · 5–6 December 2025

  • Published and presented
  • Sole author and presenter

Award Best Research Paper Presenter — Chosen from presenters representing 68 universities worldwide.

Why does a Generation Alpha viewer stop scrolling? This study introduces the VIBE framework — the visual, narrative, rhythmic and emotional cues that drive Filipino Gen Alpha's engagement with short-form video on TikTok, Instagram Reels and Facebook Reels — as a model for understanding and designing content that resonates.

FrameworkVIBEVisual Hooks · Immediate Narrative · Beat Synchrony · Emotional Relatability
Read the abstract

The rise of short-form video platforms such as TikTok, Instagram Reels and Facebook Reels has transformed media consumption among digital natives. For Generation Alpha (born 2010–2024), these platforms are not just entertainment sources but cultural touchpoints that shape perceptions, preferences and social behaviors. Unlike Millennials or Gen Z, Gen Alpha has been immersed in algorithm-driven, mobile-first ecosystems from birth, making them a unique cohort for media engagement studies (Prensky, 2021; Kaye et al., 2022).

Marketers increasingly employ “vibe marketing” — a strategy emphasizing mood, atmosphere and affective resonance rather than overt persuasion (Banet-Weiser, 2019). However, limited empirical research has mapped how specific audiovisual cues shape Gen Alpha’s decision to stop scrolling and engage with short-form video.

This study fills that gap by introducing the VIBE framework — Visual Hooks, Immediate Narrative, Beat Synchrony and Emotional Relatability — as a conceptual model for understanding and designing content that resonates with Gen Alpha. It investigates how these elements influence Filipino Gen Alpha users’ short-form video consumption, situating the findings within broader global research on digital media engagement.

January 2025 · Conference paper

A Precarious Public Service: A Case Study on the Condition of Contract of Service and Job Order Workers in a Local Government Unit in the Philippines

Society of Interdisciplinary Business Research (SIBR) Conference

Tokyo, Japan · January 2025

  • Published and presented
  • Sole author and presenter

More than half a million people work for the Philippine government as job order and contract of service workers — doing regular, necessary work without security of tenure or social protection. This case study examines their conditions in one local government unit, and the policy measures proposed to recognize their right to security of tenure.

Read the abstract

Worldwide, a great many workers face precarious, insecure and unpredictable working conditions. Unemployment figures alone fail to capture the larger number of people who work but do not have a decent job: a decent wage, a secure future, social protection and access to rights.

Contracting out — one form of privatization — refers to the state-funded delivery of public services by a private provider through a contract between the two parties. Globally, the shift from permanent to contract-based employment in the public sector is seen as an institutional response to the privatization of public services. Public choice and property rights theories view the provision of public services as a means through which officials can assert their interests and political power; Weimer and Vining (2011) attribute public-sector inefficiencies to such opportunistic behavior.

In the Philippines, three decades of austerity-driven administrative reforms have reshaped public sector employment, driven by the need to meet rising demand for public services. Job order and contract of service workers carry out regular, permanent work without guaranteed security of tenure or social protection, because no employer–employee relationship exists between them and the government. Data from the Department of Budget and Management show that the government employs more than half a million such workers — even as it requires private employers to provide minimum wages, statutory benefits and social insurance.

This study explores the present conditions of job order and contract of service workers in the Philippine government, specifically those employed in a local government unit.

July 2017 · Conference paper

Easy as ABC: A Study on How Child-Targeted Marketing Strategy Builds Brand Equity

Society of Interdisciplinary Business Research (SIBR) Conference

Osaka, Japan · July 2017

  • Published and presented
  • Lead author and presenter

Most research on child-targeted marketing focuses on its ethics. This qualitative study asked a different question — how the strategy builds brand equity — and, through interviews with marketing managers of quick-service restaurants, produced the ALPHABET framework: three steps linking children's brand awareness to financial outcomes and long-term loyalty.

FrameworkALPHABETA Learning Pattern on How to Achieve Brand Equity through Targeting children
Read the abstract

While most papers on child-targeted marketing center on the ethical issues it raises, this study explored and mapped the less-examined ways the strategy contributes to brand equity. Research suggests three components of brand equity, presented here as ABC: Authority over financial aspects, Brand awareness and Customer loyalty.

An assessment tool validated by experts was used to evaluate whether candidate companies had established brand equity. The firms that passed — all quick-service restaurants, operating as both product and service businesses — became the study’s informants. Data were gathered through interviews with their marketing managers and analyzed line by line through content analysis, then coded, categorized and clustered into emergent themes.

The findings describe three steps through which the strategy builds brand equity, presented as the ALPHABET framework (A Learning Pattern on How to Achieve Brand Equity through Targeting children):

  1. Consistently positioning the brand in children’s minds builds awareness, and children become unpaid word-of-mouth advocates among their peers.
  2. Children’s influence on family purchases gives the company greater control over financial outcomes, visible in anticipated cash flows and unplanned purchases by guardians.
  3. Together, these steps build customer loyalty — supported by the theory of planned behavior and theory of mind — which can carry into adulthood and the next generation of customers.

The study suggests start-ups, particularly quick-service restaurants, can apply the framework as a foundation for sustainable growth, and recommends further research with companies that are predominantly product- or service-based.
